Color Theory Overview

Color is more than purely a decorative element whether it pertains to design work or art. When used properly, color can hold sway over emotions and the overall feel of a painting, product, etc. Overall, color theory helps artists and designers piece together how colors work together and achieve a desired effect or feel. It breaks down how colors work in harmony and helps decide on the best color palette to utilize. Furthermore, the same color can be interpreted differently depending not only on the culture of the observer, but also on the surrounding colors adjacent to it. In product design, a good grasp on color theory can help in three main ways: create am impactful design for best visual appeal, improve UX, and lastly better communicate a brand's mood and message.

There are three main types of color in a color wheel: Primary Colors (red, blue, yellow), Secondary Colors (such as green, orange, purple), and Tertiary Colors (combinations of primary and secondary colors). Interestingly, the first color wheel, invented in the early 1600s by a Finnish atronomer named Aron Sigfrid Forsius, only included red, yellow, blue, and gray pigments. The color wheel we know and use today was inspired by Sir Isaac Newton's experimentations with light and prisms in the 1700s. His experients would become the foundation of the modern day color wheel.

Using color theory effectively also involves more than understanding the color wheel alone. Color theory also involves a knowledge of color temperature (the warmth or coolness of a color), the seven harmonious color schemes, and how to adjust the three key color elements: Hue (color), Saturation (color intensity), and Value (lightness or darkness of a color). Whether you are working on a new masterpiece, a product, or a brand, color theory is an essential tool to elevate the overall appearance of your work and how it makes observers feel towards your creation. A good grasp on this concept can enhance even a basic design into something eye-catching and inspiring.

Color Theory Terminology and Definitions

Color Wheel
First created in the 1600s and having evolved overtime into what we use today, the color wheel is an essential tool used in color theory that aids in deciding harmonious color combinations by illustrating the relationships between colors in an easy to comprehend wheel form.
RGB
Stands for Red, Green, and Blue, the three primary colors of light utilized in digital displays such as screens, monitors, and televisions. RGB is an additive color model best suited for digital media.
CMYK
Stands for Cyan, Magenta, and Black(Key) and best suited for print such business cards. CMYK is a subtracive color model and uses colors that absorb light by layering inks.
Primary Colors
Comprising of the colors red, yellow, and blue (RYB), when these base colors are combined, they create a much broader range of colors.
Secondary Colors
A range of colors that includes orange, green, and violet. Each secondary color is a combination of two primary colors. For example, red and yellow make orange.
Tertiary Colors
These colors are a result of mixing a primary color with a secondary color. Some xamples of tertiary colors are red-violet, blue-violet, and yellow-orange.
Analogous Colors
This color scheme comprises of three colors on the color wheel sitting next to each other such as voilet, blue-violet, and blue. One is chosen as the main color, the second is used as your supporting color, and the third is your accent color.
Complementary Color Scheme
Complementary colors are two colors sitting opposite of each other on the color wheel are such as yellow and purple. When used together, these colors contrast vehemently with one another. This method is useful in examples such as team colors that communicate detail even from afar thanks to high contrast.
Split-Complementary Colors
This variation on colors includes a base and complementary color in addition to the two colors on each side of the complementary color. This provides a broad color palette without the risk of clashing colors.
Monochromatic Colors
These are colors that rely heavily on shades and tinys of a single color to create a unified, unbusy look. This can range from simple grayscale to an artpiece created with only shades of purple.
Triadic Colors
This color scheme is created with three colors evenly spaced around the color wheel. A dominant color is chosen and backup up with two accent colors for a balanced, but striking result.
Tetradic Colors
This color combinarion is when you form a rectangle on the color wheel that connects two sets of complementary colors and thus balancing cool and warm tones. This color scheme provides contrast and harmony for a dramatic visual effect.
Square Colors
A color combination similar to a tetradic color scheme, but instead of a rectange, it forms a square on the color wheel.
Vibrancy
A term in color theory that refers to the brightness, intensity, and energy of a hue. Vibrant colors have the intristic ability to draw attention and thus evoke strong emotions from their percieved sense of excitement, movement, or even warmth depending on their use. They have the potiental to be dynamic or even whimsical.
Hue
Hue can be considered as the purest form of a color. It is one of the three key color elements.
Value
How much shading a color possesses. The value of a hue is referencing how much lightness or darkness that color has. Using values correctly is essential to creating contrast.
Saturation
This is the intensity and purity of a color. This element can be adjusted to enhance vibrancy or tone it down in creative work.
Contrast
This refers to how different hues interact with each other and is used to create visual harmony and even dramatic tension in artistic work. Contrast highlights the differences in a combination of colors. A good example of this can be found in complementary color schemes.
Tone
The mixture of a hue with gray. Tone can also be created by a mixture of tinting and shading.
Shade
The mixture of a hue with black, increasing its darkness.
Tint
The mixture of a hue with white, increasing its lightness.
